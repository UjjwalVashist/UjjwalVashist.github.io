Case study · Audit analytics

Audit Analytics Dashboard

Auditors can't read every booking. They run journal-entry tests, rules that pull out the postings most likely to be wrong or manipulated. This app runs nine of them from audit practice (ISA 240) on every posting, gives each flagged posting risk points, and ranks them so the auditor starts with the entries that fail the most.

Why synthetic data

Real ledgers are confidential. More importantly, with real data you can't prove a check works, because you don't know the right answer. So I generated a realistic ledger and planted known problems in it: 12 duplicate pairs, 5 gaps in document numbers, 25 weekend and holiday postings, 30 round amounts, 8 postings entered and approved by the same person, one user who books 60 invoices just under the 5,000 € approval limit, 10 post-closing entries, 15 late-night entries, 12 entries without a description – and a management-override case: the head of finance, who rarely posts, books three round 25,000 € amounts after closing, at 23:41, with no text. The tests generate fresh data with a different random seed and check that exactly these are found.

The checks

TestHow

Risk scoring

229 flagged postings are too many to check one by one. Each test has points – 3 for strong signals (duplicates, broken four-eyes principle, post-closing), 2 for medium ones, 1 for weak ones – and a posting gets the points of every test it fails: High 3+, Medium 2, Low 1. The planted management-override entries fail five tests at once and rank first with 9 points. It's the idea behind the risk-based samples in tools like MindBridge or CaseWare IDEA, kept as a transparent rule. Before any test, a population check reconciles the extract (count, total, document range, gaps), which the revised ISA 240 makes an explicit step.

The most interesting result

The whole ledger conforms to Benford's law (MAD 0.0050, "close conformity"). Looking only at the total, nothing seems wrong. Split by user, KOCH_J doesn't conform (MAD 0.0234): 17.1% of their amounts start with a 4, where 9.7% is expected. Those are the invoices kept just under the limit.

The lesson: analyse subsets such as users, vendors and accounts, because manipulation hides in the totals. Sixty postings are only 1.5% of the ledger, but a large share of one person's work.

Decisions

  • SQL for the rule checks: audit data comes from ERP databases, and SQL is what audit-analytics teams use. SQLite needs no server, so anyone can run it.
  • MAD instead of only chi-square: with thousands of postings, chi-square flags tiny, irrelevant differences. MAD doesn't depend on sample size.
  • A test flags candidates; the auditor decides. One invoice of 4,900 € means nothing. The pattern does: one user with many of them.

What I'd add next

Reading SAP tables BKPF and BSEG directly, debit and credit handling, posting-text analysis (keywords like "correction"), and vendor master checks such as a vendor sharing an employee's bank account.
