Case study · Audit analytics

Audit Analytics Dashboard

Auditors can't read every booking. They run journal-entry tests, rules that pull out the postings most likely to be wrong or manipulated. This dashboard runs seven of them on a ledger export and hands the auditor a short exception list.

Why synthetic data

Real ledgers are confidential. More importantly, with real data you can't prove a check works, because you don't know the right answer. So I generated a realistic ledger and planted known problems in it: 12 duplicate pairs, 5 gaps in document numbers, 25 weekend and holiday postings, 30 round amounts, 8 postings entered and approved by the same person, and one user who books 60 invoices just under the 5,000 € approval limit. The tests generate fresh data with a different random seed and check that exactly these are found.

The checks

TestHow
Duplicate postingsSQL GROUP BY vendor, account, amount, date and text, HAVING COUNT(*) > 1
Gaps in document numbersSQL window function LAG() compares each number with the previous one
Weekend and holiday postingspandas, with German public holidays for Baden-Württemberg
Round amountsWhole-euro amounts that divide by 1,000
Just below the approval limitAmounts from 4,500 € to just under 5,000 €, counted per user
Segregation of dutiesThe same user entered and approved the posting
Benford's lawFirst-digit shares against log10(1 + 1/d), measured with Nigrini's MAD

The most interesting result

The whole ledger conforms to Benford's law (MAD 0.0051, "close conformity"). Looking only at the total, nothing seems wrong. Split by user, KOCH_J doesn't conform (MAD 0.0232): 17.1% of their amounts start with a 4, where 9.7% is expected. Those are the invoices kept just under the limit.

The lesson: analyse subsets such as users, vendors and accounts, because manipulation hides in the totals. Sixty postings are only 1.5% of the ledger, but a large share of one person's work.

Decisions

  • SQL for the rule checks: audit data comes from ERP databases, and SQL is what audit-analytics teams use. SQLite needs no server, so anyone can run it.
  • MAD instead of only chi-square: with thousands of postings, chi-square flags tiny, irrelevant differences. MAD doesn't depend on sample size.
  • A test flags candidates; the auditor decides. One invoice of 4,900 € means nothing. The pattern does: one user with many of them.

What I'd add next

Reading SAP tables BKPF and BSEG directly, debit and credit handling, posting-text analysis (keywords like "correction"), vendor master checks, and a risk score that combines several flags per posting.
